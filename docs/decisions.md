# Decisions

The choices behind the production launch, and why. The launch plan's tracker keeps the same list (its `decisions` collection). The **Approved** ones were confirmed explicitly; the rest were proposed in the plan and are what the code does today. Each one points to where it lives.

## Product and data

**Who can sign in: an invite-only private beta, multi-tenant underneath.** Production refuses sign-in without an invite list (`AUTH_ALLOWED_EMAILS` or `AUTH_ALLOWED_DOMAINS`), and every row belongs to a user. Public sign-up is one setting, `AUTH_OPEN_SIGNUP`, off until the plans are upgraded (launch items i76, i77). With it on, at most `AUTH_SIGNUP_DAILY_LIMIT` new accounts are made a day, each user has daily quotas on what costs Atlas more than a request (CSV imports, bank links and syncs, backtests; Ask already had one) and caps on what they keep (the watchlist, journal, theses, transactions, holdings valued by hand, alert rules, and other assets and debts), and a block list ends someone's access at once. The quotas are counted in the rate limiter's Upstash store rather than in Postgres: nothing to migrate, and a Redis outage lets requests through, as it does for the rate limits. *Why:* it keeps free hosting and free market-data terms valid and limits exposure; the quotas stop a steady drip that the per-minute rate limits would let through all day.

**Linked broker accounts: one linked investment account is one portfolio.** *Approved Oct 13.* A Plaid account becomes a `portfolios` row (source, external account id). CSV and manual portfolios sit alongside it. A sync replaces holdings and upserts transactions by their external id. The all-accounts view is the sum of your portfolios, and Holdings can also show one row per symbol across them (i79). *Why:* it fits the existing table and keeps CSV import working.

**A linked account's option contracts sync with it; the plan on them stays the user's.** *(i63, i44)* Plaid's holdings that are options with their contract details become the account's option positions (source `plaid`, `src/lib/plaid/sync.ts`), replaced by each sync as its holdings are: the contract, how many and the premium are the institution's, and while the account syncs they can't be changed, moved or deleted in Atlas (`src/lib/portfolio/option-positions-store.ts`); the opened date, the user's own price, the thesis and the rules are the user's and survive every sync. A contract the user added by hand or from a CSV becomes the synced one when the institution reports it; one it doesn't report stays. Options without clear details, and option trades, aren't brought in and are counted. The institution's price is kept as a dated mark, used while no newer close or price of the user's own is stored. Options aren't yet in the portfolio value or returns, so a linked account's value is below the broker's by its options. *Why:* the ledger and the options checks should see what the account really holds, without a sync erasing the plan written for it.

**Transfers between your own accounts are worked out when read, not stored.** *(i80)* A withdrawal in one of the user's accounts and a deposit in another, of the same amount to the cent in the same currency and at most five days apart, are one transfer (`src/lib/portfolio/internal-transfers.ts`). Activity labels both sides and leaves them out of net deposits when both are shown, and Today shows one row. *Why:* it needs no migration, works the same for linked, imported and hand-entered accounts, and deleting either side ends the pairing. The cost is that two unrelated movements of the same amount within five days are paired too.

**An account's kind and ownership are what the user says.** Each account may have a type, an institution, a tax treatment and an ownership (individual, joint, for a dependent, business), all optional (`src/lib/portfolio/account-kinds.ts`). The form suggests a type's usual tax treatment and ownership, for the user to keep or change before saving. A linked account takes its type and institution from Plaid (Atlas now keeps Plaid's retirement, 529 and HSA subtypes) and the tax treatment that kind of account usually has, only until the user changes any of them or its ownership (`kind_edited_at`, `src/lib/plaid/sync.ts`). Plaid doesn't say whose name an account is in, so only the user sets ownership. Accounts splits the value by tax treatment and by ownership, with the rest under Not set and each group naming its accounts (`src/lib/portfolio/account-groups.ts`), and ownership changes no total. *Why:* a guessed tax treatment or owner would put money in the wrong group with no way to tell. The cost is that a linked account Plaid reports only as a 403(b), a 457(b) or a retirement account starts as tax-deferred even when it holds Roth money, until the user changes it; its group names it, so it shows.

**What leaves the browser: everything a user owns.** *Approved Oct 13.* Preferences and the risk policy are one versioned JSON row (`user_settings`). Theses, the journal, the watchlist, order plans, Ask conversations, valuation snapshots, strategies (i55), and alert rules with their alerts (i57) have tables of their own. Browser storage keeps only per-browser conveniences, scoped to the signed-in user. *Why:* it fixes data leaking between accounts on a shared browser, and lets Ask, alerts and the nightly jobs work on the server.

**Every figure is real or labelled.** A number comes from the user's own data, or carries a **Sample** tag and can be hidden (Settings › Data). Nothing is fabricated to fill a card: no invented deltas, sparklines, commissions or prices. *Why:* the product's first principle is truth over vibes; an investing tool that makes up numbers can't be trusted with real ones.

**Net worth: the accounts plus what the user enters by hand outside them.** Assets and debts outside the investment accounts (a home, a car, cash in the bank, a mortgage, a loan) are rows of their own (`net_worth_items`), each with a value as of a day, at most 100 per user. Each value given is kept by its day (`net_worth_item_values`), though nothing charts them yet. Once there are any, Today's headline is the net worth (portfolio value + other assets − debts), with the portfolio value beside it. Returns, the equity curve, nightly valuations, the risk policy, the weekly review, email, Ask and alerts keep measuring the portfolio value. *Why:* a user's balance sheet is more than their brokerage accounts, but a home's value is a dated guess; mixed into returns or position-sizing rules it would make them wrong. The values are kept from the first one because a value replaced before there is a history is lost, and a net-worth chart would otherwise start on the day it ships.

**A positions file's row with no ticker comes in valued by hand.** A row whose symbol isn't a ticker Atlas can price (blank, a CUSIP, a plan's number, a fund's name) but which has a market value becomes a holding valued by hand on that row's account (`manual_holdings`, source `csv`), as of the day the user picks in the preview. Within the account it is keyed by its identifier or its name, so importing the file again updates the same holding in place and records the day's value; a file older than the saved value only adds its day, a holding the next file leaves out stays, and a position an earlier import stored under its CUSIP is replaced. Without a value column such a row is an issue that says how to bring it in. A ticker Atlas has no price for stays a position valued at cost, and a linked account refuses these rows while it syncs, because its bank reports its holdings; once it no longer syncs (disconnected or revoked), a file updates the ones its last sync brought in, found by their identifier or name (`src/lib/portfolio/csvImport.ts`, `importHoldings.ts`). Each user keeps at most 1,000 holdings valued by hand; those a linked account syncs count, but a sync is never refused. *Why:* a 401(k)'s collective trust or a 529 portfolio was rejected as "not a ticker", so the account came up short by exactly the holdings that had no other way in, and a guessed ticker or price would be worse.

**No broker routing at launch.** Trade saves order plans with their rationale, checked against the user's risk policy; it never places an order. The Schwab routes stay off in production (`SCHWAB_INTEGRATION_ENABLED`). *Why:* the Schwab order route could place real orders with one global token, with no guardrails, idempotency or audit trail.

**Ask: deterministic tools first, Claude on top.** Ask answers from the user's own data with a fixed set of tools (`POST /api/ask`), 30 questions a day. With `ASK_MODEL` set (i59), Claude reads the question, calls the same tools through AI Gateway and writes a few sentences, with the tools' rows listed under them; the answer is kept only if every figure in it is one a tool returned, and otherwise, or when the gateway fails, the rules planner answers. The gateway routes only to providers with zero data retention that don't train on prompts. Each question is bounded (4 model calls, 6 tool calls, 2,000 output tokens each, 25 s), so the daily limit bounds each user's cost: a question takes its place in the limit before it's answered, so questions sent at once can't be answered past it. *Why:* the rules cost nothing and need no model, so the LLM is an upgrade, not a dependency; checking its figures keeps Ask's answers to the user's own numbers.

**Alpha Radar is shared, and admins manage it.** The tracked filers and their 13F data are the same for every user; only `AUTH_ADMIN_EMAILS` may add, change or refresh them (i69). Each user follows the filers they want, and Today shows those (i60); each change shows where it meets the user's holdings, watchlist and theses (i61). A filing's information table is read from the attachment SEC's index names, and its holdings and report are kept as evidence, searchable by keyword and, with `ALPHA_RADAR_EMBEDDING_MODEL`, by meaning through AI Gateway, routed only to providers that keep nothing and don't train on it. The embeddings are stored with each chunk as JSON, so Postgres needs no extension (i62). *Why:* every refresh calls SEC EDGAR, and one user's change reaches everyone.

**Holdings shows positions worth very little as one row, and only there.** A position worth under $500 and under 0.5% of the market value of every position held, whatever the filter, is small. Three or more are one row after the others that opens in place, never every position shown, and By symbol judges a symbol across its accounts. It changes only what the table draws: every total, the risk policy, Today, Ask and the export count each position. There is no setting and nothing is stored (`src/lib/holdings/small-positions.ts`). *Why:* a robo-advisor's dozens of fractional positions buried the ones that matter. The dollar limit keeps real money in view in a large book, the share limit keeps a small book's positions in view, and a display choice that changes no figure doesn't need a settings version.

## Infrastructure

**Hosting: Vercel Hobby during the beta, Pro before public or paid use.** The Docker image stays as a portable fallback (`README.md` › Running the production image). *Why:* native Next.js 16, a preview per pull request, cron and environment management built in. Hobby is for non-commercial use, and its cron runs once a day.

**Database: the existing Supabase project is production; local development and E2E use Docker Postgres.** Nothing local ever points at Supabase. *Why:* the free organisation already had two active projects, and local development writing to production through the sign-in bypass had to stop.

**The app connects with least privilege.** At runtime the app is a member of `app_runtime` (migration 0010), with only the grants it needs. The table owner is used only by migrations, from CI. *Why:* a bug or injection in the app can't alter the schema or reach what it was never granted (`docs/local-database-secrets.md`).

**Sign-in: Auth.js with Google and database sessions.** Sessions live in `auth_sessions`, not in a signed cookie. *Why:* deleting an account or removing someone from the invite list ends their sessions straight away.

**Market data: Massive Basic, stored in Postgres.** The nightly ingest stores each trading day's closes from Massive's grouped daily bars (one call for every US stock), and pages read prices from there, never from Massive. History (the sparklines on Holdings and Today, the thesis charts) reads the same store's daily bars (x09) once the ingest has backfilled a symbol's two years, which can take several nights for a new symbol (a dozen or so backfills a weekday run, held symbols and benchmarks first); until then, or for a symbol the store doesn't track, a range its stored bars don't cover asks Massive, rather than chart a few days as the range. A symbol whose backfill found nothing (a fund Massive doesn't price) answers empty without asking again. That, fundamentals and the nightly ingest share a budget of 5 Massive calls a minute. Ticker search (i35) reads a directory of listed US companies that the nightly job copies from SEC EDGAR's free ticker file, plus the benchmark ETFs and the user's own holdings: search-as-you-type would spend Massive's whole minute in a few keystrokes, and SEC's file costs one request a night. Alpha Vantage was dropped. During the session, Finnhub's free quotes take the closes' place (i33): one row per symbol in `intraday_quotes`, fetched about every 80 seconds by whichever instance claims it, and labelled "delayed" with its time, never live. *Why:* the grouped call fits the free plan's limit. Finnhub prices one symbol per call, 60 calls a minute for the whole app, so its quotes are shared in Postgres, where the claims under an advisory lock also count the app's calls; a Redis cache and budget would spend Upstash's free commands every minute of the session. Both free tiers are for non-commercial use, so the plan is revisited before public launch (i76).

**Backtests: daily bars Atlas stores, rules it can compute.** *Decided Nov 3 (i55).* A strategy's rules compare indicators worked out from daily bars (Close, SMA, EMA, RSI, momentum, dollar volume) with numbers or each other; its universe is a list of up to 25 tickers. A backtest runs on the server over up to two years of stored closes: long only, signals at a close traded at the next open, no fees, and SPY over the same window beside it. Fundamentals rules and index-membership filters (the old builder's P/E and "S&P 500") are gone. *Why:* Atlas stores prices but no fundamentals or index constituents, and fetching them would spend the free market-data budget; a backtest that silently ignored a rule would be worse than no rule. Saved as JSON on the strategy, so the vocabulary can grow without a migration.

**Email: Resend, opt-in, its own table.** *Decided Nov 3 (i58).* Alerts and the weekly review go by email only to users who turn them on in Settings; both are off by default, including for accounts that had the old "Weekly Summary" switch on (it never sent anything). The choices and each user's unsubscribe token live in `email_preferences`, not in the `user_settings` document. Every email has a one-click unsubscribe (RFC 8058) that needs only the link's token; the footer link opens a page with a button, since mail scanners open links. Alerts go in one email per evening; Alpha Radar alerts go with the rest rather than through Alpha Radar's own Email channel, which is gone. Subjects carry no amounts. *Why:* the browser writes the settings document back whole, so a tab holding the old one would turn an email back on after an unsubscribe; opt-in keeps the beta from emailing anyone unasked; and Resend is called with one `fetch`, no SDK.

**Background jobs: daily Vercel Crons.** `GET /api/cron/daily` runs the jobs in order (sessions, Ask history, Plaid, prices, snapshots, alerts, email) behind `CRON_SECRET`, and `GET /api/cron/alpha-radar` runs Alpha Radar's SEC job each morning on its own (launch item i60), so SEC never takes time from the price backfill (`docs/deployment.md` › Scheduled jobs). Supabase `pg_cron` calling a secured route is the option if intraday alerts need it. *Why:* Hobby runs a cron job once a day, within about an hour of its time, and gives each route at most five minutes; it allows 100 of them.

**Deploys go through CI.** Vercel's Git integration deploys nothing: not `master`, and no previews of pull requests, which CI's E2E build already covers and which used up the Hobby plan's 100 deployments a day. The `deploy-production` job deploys only after every check passes: it applies migrations first, then deploys, then smoke-tests the live site. Migrations are add-first, because the old code is still running while they apply (`docs/deployment.md`). *Why:* nothing reaches production untested, or without its migrations.

**Shared rate limits in Upstash Redis.** Limits apply per IP and per user, in every instance alike, and are namespaced per environment. Without Upstash each process keeps its own. *Why:* serverless instances come and go; a per-instance limit protects nothing.

**A nonce-based Content-Security-Policy.** The proxy gives every response a fresh script nonce with `'strict-dynamic'`, so no injected script runs (i26). *Why:* the app shows financial data and opens Plaid Link; an XSS bug shouldn't be able to run code.

**Backups: a nightly encrypted dump, restored every time.** A GitHub workflow dumps production, encrypts it with `age` and keeps it as an artifact for 30 days. In the same run it restores the dump into a scratch database and checks every table (`docs/backups.md`). *Why:* Supabase's free plan has no point-in-time recovery, and a backup nobody has restored isn't a backup.

**Monitoring: a scheduled GitHub workflow that keeps one issue open.** Every half hour it checks `/api/health/ready` and `/api/health/jobs`, opens an issue that mentions the owner when something fails, and closes it once everything passes (`docs/monitoring.md`). Errors go to Sentry. *Why:* it's free, needs no new service, and alerts once per outage rather than every half hour.
